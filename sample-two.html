DANDANI: THE SPIRIT HEIR OF VEYLORIA · A FREE SAMPLE

Chapter 1

The Woman Who Should Not Be Here

The realm does not ask the stranger to become Veylorian. It asks only whether the stranger can enter without forgetting the shape of the life that brought her there. For those who forget themselves, even paradise becomes another kind of exile.

— From the Third Record of the House of Remembering

Light did not rise in Veyloria. It breathed. It came across the sky in long swells that lifted the floating mountains out of the dark one at a time and left the silver rivers for last, and between one swell and the next the whole realm went still, as if waiting to be told the day could begin. There was no dawn. Goci had grown up with a clean line between night and morning. Here the line was a loosening, violet giving way to gold at the edge of the world, the dark thinning more than lifting.

She stood on a crystal balcony with one hand on the rail. The rail had not been there the evening before. The palace had grown it overnight the way a vine finds a wall, curling to the height of whoever would lean on it. Behind her, the room she had slept in hummed in its stone whenever she breathed too deeply. She had stopped breathing deeply in it.

Her fingers went to her shoulders to draw the shawl closer and found only her own collarbones. The shawl had gone into the light at the crossing, and her hands had not yet accepted it. The motion was older than Dandani, older than the road to Dandani, made every morning since before she could remember choosing it. The air held no chill. She let her hands fall. Everything in this realm was either grown or kept, and she was neither. She did not belong here.

Solan did.

He crossed the crystal floor toward her, and the word she wanted for how he moved no longer existed. He had stopped toddling weeks ago. At two months old he walked with a level, steady tread, his silver-touched eyes taking in the morning light and holding it, the way certain stones keep the heat of a day after the sun is gone.

“Mother.” His voice was clear and unhurried. “The sky is awake.”

Her chest tightened, and she smiled over it. “Yes, baby. It is.”

He reached up for her hand. The Binding mark on her wrist pulsed once under his fingers, warm and immediate. It did not hurt. It had never hurt. It answered him first, every time, before her mind had finished noticing he was there.

Avelon stood in the doorway behind them, tall and barefoot, wearing the look she had learned to read over these weeks. It was not sorrow and not fear. It was the smooth, worn place where both had been held back for years.

“You should eat,” he said. “The trials begin today.”

“Trials.”

He crossed the warm stone without a sound. “The Council must see whether you can govern the Binding. If they judge that you cannot...” He let the sentence stop there. It ended well enough without him.

“I’m not leaving Solan.”

“I know.”

“Mother stays,” Solan told the room, with approval.

Avelon looked down at the boy, and an expression crossed his face that Goci still had no name for. It had awe in it, and dread braided through the awe. “He grows stronger each day. Too strong, some would say. Faster than the old stories allowed.”

She lifted her chin. The defiance came up easily now, familiar as breath. “Then they will learn to accept him.”

“Not every spirit wants him alive.”

She stood with that a moment before she trusted her voice. Avelon stepped closer and lowered his own until it seemed meant for her alone. “Whatever the trials ask of you, be ready to give it.”

Goci looked past him, out at the peaks and the rivers climbing toward a sky that had not yet committed to day. “I didn’t survive Dandani to lose him here.”

The mark pulsed under her skin. Far across the valley the light swelled once and went still, as if the realm had heard her and was thinking it over.

*  *  *

Her first night in the palace had not felt like a night. She had stood at the open window long after Solan fell asleep, watching light travel across the far hills. No moon she knew hung in that sky, and still the land never darkened. A pale glow rose out of the earth itself and silvered the trees and drew the rivers in thin, exact lines, as if someone had inked the whole valley by hand and left the ink wet.

She had laid her palm on the windowsill. The stone was warm.

That frightened her more than cold would have. In the human world a stone was a stone. A wall did not remember the hands that built it. A doorway did not breathe. Here nothing she touched was only itself, and by the second hour she had begun to suspect the realm was a single creature that had agreed, for now, to keep its shape still enough for people to walk through.

“You’re listening again.”

Avelon stood a few paces behind her, one shoulder silvered by the glow.

“To what?”

“Everything.”

“I don’t know how to stop.”

“You may never stop.”

She turned all the way around at that. The words had something under them, and she wanted to see his face. “You knew this would happen.”

He took his time. He chose his next sentence the way a man counts coins he cannot spare. “I knew that bringing you here would wake things that had slept a very long time. I did not know how fast, or how far.”

“And Solan?”

The old fear surfaced in his face, and he put it down again. “Solan most of all.”

“Then tell me the truth.”

“The truth is rarely one thing, in a realm built like this one.”

He came to the window. She felt the heat of him, separate from the heat of the stone. “Veyloria has waited a long time for someone who could stand between worlds without belonging to either.”

“And you think that’s me.”

“I think the realm has already answered. Whether we are ready for the answer is another matter.”

*  *  *

She slept badly after that. Solan’s breath at her side was the only familiar thing in the room, and toward morning it carried her down into a doze too thin to call rest.

Warmth came into it, and it was not the palace’s warmth. A woman’s voice hummed a low melody with no words she could catch, though she reached for them the way you reach for a name on the edge of memory. A hand that was not Avelon’s, not Mara’s, not any she could place, rested on her hair and smoothed it back, the way a mother does before waking a child. And underneath, so faint she could never afterward swear she had heard it and not merely felt its shape, there was one word. It was not quite a name.

Sister.

She woke with her heart racing and her eyes wet. The room was still. Solan slept on. She told no one, not Avelon, not the watchful spirits who moved through the palace like drafts. She told herself what she had told herself in other strange beds: a tired mind in an unfamiliar room could conjure anything, and not every ache needed digging to its root.

The dream came twice more, never longer, never clearer. Each time she woke, her hand had already gone to the foot of the bed, where the shawl used to lie folded, and she lay with her fingers closed on the blanket without knowing why.

She thought of writing it down. Once that had been her only safe place for a feeling, a page nobody else would open. But the paper in Veyloria was made to last, and whatever was written on it would be kept, and she was not ready to let the realm keep this.

Solan found her at the edge of the bed the morning after the second dream. He studied her with the gravity he saved for rooms where something unnamed was sitting.

“You’re listening again,” he said. They were the same words Avelon had used, and he had no way of knowing it.

“Am I?”

He climbed up beside her and settled his small warm weight against her ribs. “To the warm thing.”

Goci went still. “What warm thing, sweetheart?”

He took his time, as he did with most answers. “The one that sings.”

“Do you hear it too?”

“Sometimes.” He picked at a loose thread on her sleeve. “It’s not scary. It’s waiting.”

“For what?”

He looked up. His eyes were calm in a way that unsettled her more than fear would have. “I don’t know yet. It likes you.”

She held him tighter than the moment needed. He was two months old. She would not press him for answers she suspected he did not have. She put the words away with the others, the ones she had learned not to throw out just because they came without explanation.

*  *  *

She was still on the balcony when she heard small footsteps behind her. Solan came carrying a silver cup in both hands, his whole body given over to the work of not spilling it.

“Drink,” he said, and held it up.

“Who gave you that?”

He pointed at the doorway with his chin, since his hands were full. “The lady with no shadow.”

“There are ladies with no shadow?”

He nodded, solemn. To him it was only a fact. “She likes you.”

Goci crouched and took the cup. The water looked ordinary. The moment her fingers closed on the silver, a warm pulse ran up through her palm, curious, like a question asked in a language it had not finished learning.

“Did she say what it is?”

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

He weighed it. “It knows.”

She nearly laughed. She looked into the corridor instead. “Avelon?”

No answer. She set the cup on the stone rail. “Stay here a moment.”

Solan frowned. “Mother goes?”

“Only to the doorway. I can see you.”

“Mother stays,” he said again, with the stubbornness of a child who has found a phrase that works and means to wear it out.

She found Avelon halfway down the corridor, speaking low to an old spirit built from layers of glass, each layer catching the light at its own angle. The spirit saw her and withdrew into the wall the way mist leaves a field when the sun clears the trees.

“You were talking about me.”

He did not deny it. “I was talking about what happens if the Council decides you cannot govern the Binding.”

“Then tell me what failure looks like.”

He was silent a beat too long.

“That bad?”

“It depends what you mean by failure.”

“Losing control of it. Hurting someone. Hurting Solan. Hurting myself.”

His eyes went toward the room where their son waited. “The Binding is not a weapon. It answers what you believe you are protecting, in the moment you believe it most. If your fear outgrows your judgment, it may decide for you.”

“That is not comforting.”

“It was never meant to be.”

She read the stillness he kept on his face like a locked door. “You know more than you’re telling me. You have since the night we crossed.”

A small change moved through his expression. “You’re right.”

“About what?”

“That I know more than I have said.”

The admission sat between them, heavier than its length. “Then don’t let me learn this place while everyone in it already knows it,” she said. “I’ve spent enough of my life being the last to understand my own story.”

He flinched, barely. “For years I thought the less you knew, the safer you’d be.”

“And now?”

“Now I think that’s a danger of its own.” His jaw worked. “The slow kind.”

A bell sounded beneath the palace. It was not loud, only a single deep note that passed through the walls instead of around them and arrived inside her chest. Solan had followed her to the doorway. He turned his face toward it.

“The Hall is calling.”

Avelon stared down at him. “How do you know that?”

Solan seemed puzzled by the question. “It told me.”

She took his hand to lead him in. On the balcony behind them the silver cup stood empty on the rail, though neither of them had drunk from it. Beyond it, in the strongest light of the morning, a woman stood in the doorway of the far gallery with nothing at her feet, no shadow on the floor at all. She inclined her head, once, the way a servant does, or a guest. Then Solan tugged Goci’s sleeve, and when she looked back the doorway was only a doorway.

*  *  *

Before the first trial, Avelon took her down to the silver river. He wanted her to understand one true thing about Veyloria before she stood in front of the Council and pretended to understand the rest.

“Humans misread one rule here,” he said as they went down the narrow path. “Nothing in this realm moves without remembering where it has been.”

At the bank he told her to watch. The river climbed a low slope, curved around the base of a floating garden, and poured straight up into the sky. Gravity, on this stretch, seemed to have been offered the chance to decline and had taken it.

“How is that possible?”

“You’re asking the wrong question. Ask where it believes it is going.”

“Water doesn’t believe.”

“In your world.”

He crouched and touched the surface with two fingers. The water rose around his hand and curled and pooled, and she knew the look of it, because it was recognition. She reached toward it. It drew back into itself before she could touch it.

“It doesn’t like me.”

“It doesn’t know you. The difference matters more here than anywhere.”

“That’s a very personal thing for a river.”

“It is personal. Nothing here answers command, not for long. Trust does more than strength.”

She looked over the folded country, the floating gardens, the mountains that owed nothing to the ground. “Then maybe the Council is afraid because it can’t command everything either.”

Avelon’s mouth moved toward a real smile. “Now you are beginning to understand this place.”

She turned back to the river and did not reach for it. She let her hand rest open above the surface, asking nothing. The water came up on its own. A thin ribbon of it wound around her fingers, warm instead of wet, and held.

Avelon said nothing. The river had made its first choice about her.

*  *  *

She learned in those first days that the palace kept people by the sounds they left behind. A corridor near her room filled with footsteps whenever she walked it, though no one else was ever there. A doorway warmed when Solan approached, bracing for the pleasure of him. A room in the eastern wing held the faint echo of a woman’s laughter, and no living spirit would say whose.

“Veyloria records what happens inside it,” Avelon told her when she asked.

“Everything?”

“Only what matters enough to be kept.”

“Who decides that?”

“The realm. Not the Council. Not, I suspect, even the oldest of the spirits who serve it.”

She liked the answer less than she showed.

One afternoon, while Solan napped under Avelon’s watch, she found a room of small things laid out on low stone shelves. A carved wooden horse missing an ear. A broken crown whose jewels had long since gone. A faded blue scarf, a cracked bowl, a pressed flower gone brittle with age, a single silver shoe with no mate. Nothing in it was magical. All of it had been loved.

“What is this place?” she said aloud.

An old spirit appeared in the doorway, the color of aged parchment. “The room of what was carried.”

“Carried where?”

“Across time.” When she pressed, it explained that whenever someone left Veyloria for good, by death, by exile, by the long wearing of years, the realm kept one ordinary object that had been theirs. Never the greatest achievement or the proudest title. Something plain.

“Why an ordinary thing?”

“So that we remember they were people before they became history.”

She thought of Dandani, of how many of its names had worn down to rumor and caution. “Does it keep human things too?”

The spirit studied her. “It has begun to.”

Her chest tightened. “Because of me?”

“Because of the child.”

On a lower shelf lay a small blue ribbon, much newer than anything around it, its color still bright. She touched it. The whole palace hummed in answer, a low sound felt more than heard. She did not know whose ribbon it was, or whose life it had been set aside to remember. The realm had simply begun making room for a memory that did not exist yet, and she found that harder to stand beside than the trials.

She thought of Mara. Of the cottage, Sera’s archive, the fountain that had shown her so much before she knew how to read it. Was there a room like this in the House of Remembering? Had Mara felt the gap yet, or was it still on its way, the way grief arrives late and all at once? Time ran strangely across the veil. She could not tell herself when she would stand in that cottage again, or whether. She had left people before. She knew the sound of a door closing softly, which was worse than a slam. She did not know whether this one would open from the other side.

*  *  *

The melody stayed with her. She had no words for it and no notes written down, only its shape, a rise and a settling, and by the third day she caught herself humming it over the washbasin and once, startled, in the middle of a sentence to Avelon, who pretended not to notice.

She tried it alone, in the corridor outside her room, where the walls kept the sound of footsteps. She hummed the first phrase under her breath and stopped.

The walls hummed it back.

It came a half-step wrong, thin and uncertain, a stranger repeating a word in a language it had only overheard. She stood very still. The corridor waited. She sang the phrase again, a little louder, and this time the echo returned true, note for note, warm in the stone. Beneath it, so low she felt it in her teeth, ran a second line she had not sung.

She stopped, and the corridor stopped with her.

She stood there a long moment with her palm against the wall and her heart going fast. Then she walked back to the room. She did not hum again that day. She did not tell Avelon, and told herself that was because it was a small thing.

It was not a small thing. She knew that, the way she had known the stone was warm.

*  *  *

The way to breakfast ran through a long gallery where servant spirits stood at intervals along the wall, each holding a small, patient light. They had bowed to Goci every day, a shallow dip she had learned to return with a nod. For Avelon they did something else. As he passed, each spirit lowered its light a full hand’s breadth, and the floor, which hummed under everyone, went almost silent beneath his bare feet.

She noticed on the third bow. She was still counting when the fourth spirit, younger than the rest and visibly nervous, opened its mouth.

“My—”

“Avelon will do,” Avelon said, without breaking stride.

The spirit’s light flickered, mortified, and it bowed lower still, which only made things worse.

“They like to be correct,” he said when they were out of earshot. He said it lightly. It did not quite work.

“Correct about what?”

“Courtesy. Veylorians keep a great many rules about who bows to whom. I stopped keeping them a long time ago.”

“They haven’t.”

“No.” He looked down the gallery and not at her. “They haven’t.”

She let it lie. She had spent too many years being asked questions she did not want to answer to press someone else for his. But she added the word the young spirit had swallowed to the others she was learning not to dismiss: the warm thing that sang, the lady with no shadow, and now a floor that held its breath for a man who said it was only manners.

At breakfast she noticed the palace had started treating her as family. A chair had appeared beside Avelon’s place without anyone announcing it. A bowl of fruit waited at Solan’s seat. Beside her own plate sat a small dish of bread, plain and a little uneven, the way only hand-shaped bread ever is. It looked exactly like the bread of the life she had left.

She touched it. “Who made this?”

A servant spirit smiled. “The kitchen.”

“There’s a kitchen here that knows human bread?”

“It knows you now.”

Avelon shrugged, with an air of private pleasure. “I told them what you had been missing.”

It nearly undid her. She had spent weeks defending her right to stay in this place, and she had forgotten how it felt to be welcomed.

It nearly undid her. Weeks of defending her right to be here, and nobody had thought to simply offer her bread.

She laughed, surprised at how easily it came. “Yes. I am.”

The word was unfamiliar in her mouth and not unwelcome. Avelon watched her. “You could be happy here. Not just safe.”

“I know that now.”

“But?”

“Happiness doesn’t undo where I came from.”

“I would never want it to.”

Solan had brought his own bread to the table. He drew it out from inside his shirt, a small loaf in a stained cloth, gone hard as a river stone, and set it beside the kitchen’s soft, uneven bread with the care of a man laying down a rival’s offering.

“You can have the new one,” Goci said. “That one is days old.”

“It’s Lily’s.” He rested one hand on the cloth. “She said it was better. I have to find out.”

“Then eat it.”

“Not yet.” He considered the matter with the gravity of a magistrate. “It’s for later.”

She did not ask later than what. In a palace that kept one plain object for everyone who left, she understood the loaf better than she wanted to.

Solan chose that moment to drop a piece of bread. It bounced once off the table’s edge and vanished into a pocket of light that had opened beneath the table without being asked.

Solan stared. Goci stared. Avelon sighed. “The palace is still learning table manners.”

She laughed with nothing held back, the first time since she crossed. The sound carried through the room, and somewhere beyond the walls a bell answered it once.

*  *  *

The palace had laid a robe out for the trials: grey, undyed, plain as a novice’s habit, folded on the chair beside the washbasin. Goci put it on and stood before the long glass and did not know what to do with her hands.

They went to her shoulders, as they did every morning, and found the robe’s bare collar. She let them fall. She tried again, and made herself look at the woman in the glass. The robe was cut high at the neck and hung straight, and nothing in it gave her anything to pull close. She was a person with nothing at her back.

“It isn’t cold here,” Avelon said from the doorway.

“I know.”

He had his cloak over one arm, the dark one that had dripped on her step in Dandani. He held it out and did not come forward, leaving the distance for her to cross or not.

She looked at it. She wanted, badly, to take it. The wool was the weight of the one she had hung beside her stove, and it would smell of him, and for the length of the trial she would have something warm at her shoulders that someone had chosen to give her.

“No,” she said. “Thank you.”

He lowered it without argument. “It was not a bargain.”

“I know. If I take it, I will start to need it.” She tried a smile. “I have only just learned how to stop needing the other one.”

He crossed the room then and stopped behind her, and for a moment did nothing at all. She watched him in the glass: the careful stillness, the hand that rose and halted an inch from her shoulder. Then, with the lightest pressure, he laid his palm between her shoulder blades, over the plain grey cloth, and left it there.

It was not much. It was warm and steady and asked for nothing. She breathed out, and the knot she had carried since waking loosened a turn.

“Mother.”

Solan stood in the doorway with a crust of the kitchen’s bread in his fist. He looked at her shoulders, and then at her.

“Where is your blanket?”

“It went into the light,” Goci said. “When we came here. It’s gone, sweetheart.”

“It’s not gone.” He said it with the patience of a child correcting a grown person’s sums. “It went where things are kept.”

Avelon’s hand went still on her back.

“Where is that?” she asked.

Solan considered it. “Under,” he said, which was no answer at all, and went back to his crust.

*  *  *

Avelon stopped her at the threshold before she left for the trials. He asked it low, as if it mattered more to him than anything the Council would put to her that day.

“What do you want from Veyloria?”

She had expected power, or safety, or survival. The question made her think of home instead, whatever shape that might still take.

“I want Solan to grow up without being afraid of what he is.”

“And for yourself?”

She looked at the mountains catching the early gold. “I want to stop feeling that I have to prove I deserve to exist somewhere. Anywhere.”

He took her hand. “You never had to prove it to me. Not once.”

“I know,” she said, and was a little surprised to find she did.

*  *  *

The Hall of Dawn stood above the older Hall of Echoes, where she had first faced the Council. Behind her, Solan slept in the room where the servant spirits had learned, in a few days, exactly how she liked her bread. Ahead waited the Council, and whatever judgment Veyloria had already started forming about her.

She pressed her palm to the doors’ frame. “I am still human,” she said, low, because she needed to hear it said before she went in.

The wood warmed under her hand and neither agreed nor argued.

Avelon stood beside her. “Nothing here can take that from you.”

“Not even the Binding?”

“Not unless you surrender it. It does not take. It only answers what it is given.”

She nodded. The dream still lingered at the edge of her thoughts, the warm word she could not remember hearing and could not set down. Behind them, far off, she thought she heard Solan stir and murmur in his sleep, a fragment of melody or nothing at all. She chose not to go back and find out which.

She opened the door.
