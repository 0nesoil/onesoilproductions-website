DANDANI: THE VEIL BETWEEN · A FREE SAMPLE

Chapter 1

Arrival

Every town keeps two kinds of history. One it tells at the fire. One it buries beneath the floor.

— fragment, the Second Archive, hand unknown

Merchants in Limodi State liked to say that fate was a guardrail, and they kept to the wide northern road, where caravans rolled smoothly in both directions. The southern road into Dandani was rough and unpaved, its edges crowded with thistle and a pale flowering weed the local children called sun-tears. That afternoon, a woman left the smooth road and took the southern one. She did not know she was fulfilling one of her prophecies, or what Dandani would ask of her.

Her name was Goci. She carried a small leather satchel in one hand and wore a silver-threaded shawl loosely around her shoulders, and she walked easily, without hurry, a gait she had practiced for years because it was the best way to hide the fact that every instinct she had was telling her to run. The low sun caught the threads in the shawl and turned them pale and cold, as if the cloth hadn’t decided which world it belonged to. Below her, Dandani spread out: grey stone and dark timber, smoke climbing straight up in the still air, and beyond the rooftops, the palace, its towers holding the last of the gold light.

She had never been here. She had made sure of that, poring over her maps for three days before she chose it, because none of them had anything to say about the town beyond its name. Even so, it rose to meet her with a strange familiarity. It didn’t feel welcoming. It felt like being waited for. The feeling settled in her chest the way cold gets into a room through a window left open a crack, and by the time she thought to shut it, it was already in.

She dismissed the thought as ordinary, like every other thought of its kind she had learned to ignore. She hadn’t come to Dandani looking for answers. She had come for anonymity: a grey, forgettable life in which nobody had a reason to look at her twice. She wanted to be the woman who bought bread at the same hour every morning and was known for nothing else: not for her name, not for the places she had lived, and not for the thing under her ribs that she had spent years teaching to lie still.

Dandani was a small town with a proud bearing, wearing its modest history like a coat cut for someone grander. Stone houses lined the main street, their windows framed in carved wooden symbols from the old traditions of Limodi State: spirals inside spirals, a stylized eye, a crescent moon lying on its back like a cradle. The marketplace was winding down after a day’s trading. The air was thick with cardamom and dried chili and a sweeter smell underneath that she couldn’t place. Indigo and rust cloth hung from the stalls, carved wooden figurines stood in rows beside baskets of fruit going soft in the last of the heat, and over it all drifted the scent of roasted tamari nuts from a brazier someone was already banking for the night.

What caught her attention, once she looked past the trading, was how little of the town had been built for the present. New roofs sat on old walls. Doorways had been widened by generations of shoulders, then narrowed again by families who decided a smaller opening was safer. The stone steps were worn into shallow bowls by feet no one alive could have named. There were no grand monuments, no statue of a founder, nothing announcing an ancient civilization. The town’s age lived in small things: a lintel with three symbols so worn they looked like natural flaws in the stone; a woman sweeping her threshold who paused to touch one of them before going in; an old man at the corner of the market mending a stool with a tool whose handle had been polished smooth by hands that might have been his grandfather’s, or his grandfather’s grandfather’s.

She had seen old towns before, and they usually wore their age like a boast. Dandani wore its like a secret. The difference stuck with her.

She walked into the marketplace and the town noticed. She had watched it happen in a dozen other towns, in a dozen other lives, and it still startled her a little, the way a ripple crosses still water faster than it should. A merchant stopped halfway through a sentence, his mouth open around the next word. Three young men throwing dice by the well turned together, as though she had called their names. An elderly woman leaned to her companion and murmured behind a spotted hand, eyes on Goci’s face the whole time. Even the guard at the palace road, a boy of nineteen at most, glanced at her with open unease and went through the gates faster than he had come.

She kept walking, chin level, gaze soft and unfocused, the look of someone who noticed nothing and cared less. Being noticed was nothing new. It was an old weight she had carried so long it had grown comfortable, like a scar that only ached in certain weather. The ache had no single source, only a long accumulation of partings, each taking a piece of her that never grew back. What bothered her was how organized the noticing was. The talk moved with a rhythm, as if the town had rehearsed this moment before she was born.

The fountain she was passing was dry, its basin furred with green, a single copper coin at the bottom like a wish nobody had quite had the nerve to make. A man stepped into her path. He was tall and broad-shouldered, dressed a shade too well for a market at closing time. He had the easy stance of someone used to being looked at, and he looked mildly put out that for once the square’s attention was on somebody else.

“Good evening,” he said, with a smile that had clearly worked on many women, none of whom had spent ten years learning to read a smile the way a soldier reads a battlefield. “We don’t get many new—”

“Only passing through.” Goci gave him the smallest nod that still counted as courtesy.

He laughed softly, as if she’d made a joke at both their expense. “Passing through?”

“Yes.”

“No one passes through Dandani.” His smile widened, and something in it had turned. It wasn’t menace, exactly, but it was sure of itself in a way she liked less than menace. “They stay.”

She looked at him a second longer than the conversation needed, fixing his face in her memory as she did with every face that might matter. Harmless, perhaps. Worth remembering. Then she walked on without a word. His puzzled look followed her across the square, close behind the talk.

The cottage she had rented sat at the edge of town, and she reached it well before dark. It was small, built of pale stone gone soft at the edges, with a blue door faded nearly to grey and an unlit lantern hanging over it. She set down her satchel and laid a hand on the doorframe. The wood was warm, warmer than the cooling evening had any right to leave it.

Beside the door, almost hidden under the weathering, was a shallow carving: a circle crossed by a single vertical line, with two smaller marks beneath. She touched it with one fingertip. The stone was cold. Then, for an instant, it wasn’t; it warmed under her hand, and she drew her fingers back.

Across the lane an old man sat under a fig tree. He had stopped watching the road and was watching her. When their eyes met he didn’t look away. He raised one finger to his lips.

Goci frowned. The old man lowered his hand and went back to his work as if nothing had happened. When she looked at the carving again it was only stone.

The old man’s finger stayed with her, and her thoughts went back to Mara, the small, silver-haired woman who had arranged the rental and met her at the town gate that afternoon with a brass key and a manner too brisk for chat. Mara had counted the coins without comment, though her eyes had rested on the threads of Goci’s shawl.

“That’s an unusual piece,” she’d said.

“It was my mother’s.”

Something in the older woman’s face had shifted, very slightly. “Then keep it close.” Before Goci could ask why, Mara had pressed the key into her palm and rattled off the particulars: well out back, market shuts at dusk, second latch sticks. Then, as if it had only just occurred to her, “Bells after midnight, don’t go out.”

“Bells?”

Mara shrugged. “Wind plays tricks. Lift the latch before you pull.”

The answer hadn’t satisfied Goci at the gate, and it satisfied her even less now that she stood at the very door the warning had been about.

Inside, the cottage smelled of lavender and old books, someone’s careful housekeeping laid over years of use. She left her things beside the bed and went to the window, palms on the sill. Dandani was changing with the evening. Lanterns came on one by one along the streets, more like something waking than something being lit. Merchants shuttered their stalls with the unhurried clatter of people who had done it at the same hour for years. Children were called home by name. The sky went from gold to violet to the deep blue that comes just before dark.

This was what she’d wanted: quiet, and a place to sink into ordinary life until she became no one in particular.

She closed her eyes. For a few seconds there was real silence, the kind she hadn’t had in longer than she cared to count. Then a slight pressure gathered under her ribs, low and warm, like a hand laid flat against her from the inside. Not pain. Not quite fear. Something old and distant, and, worst of all, nearly glad.

She opened her eyes. It was gone as fast as it had come, leaving the ordinary ache of a day’s walking and the smell of lavender.

Before she could make anything of it there was a knock, hesitant, and then a second knock, softer, as if the first had used up the courage the second one needed. On the threshold stood a young girl clutching a small basket of fruit against her hip with both arms, the way a much smaller child carries something precious.

“My mother says you must be hungry,” the girl said, not quite meeting her eyes. “She says newcomers should be welcomed while the market’s still open and there’s fresh fruit left.”

Something in Goci’s chest loosened at the plain kindness of it. “That’s very kind. Thank you.”

The girl handed over the basket: plums, a few late figs, and a single bruised apple set on top as though she were embarrassed by it. Then she stayed where she was, shifting from one bare foot to the other.

“Is there something else?”

The girl looked up at her fully for the first time, with the frank, merciless curiosity children allow themselves. “Are you staying long?”

“I’m not sure yet.” It was the truest thing she’d said since crossing the town line, and she hadn’t planned to say it.

The girl nodded, as if this confirmed what she’d already guessed. She lowered her voice and glanced over her shoulder at the darkening lane. “Everyone’s talking about you.”

Goci’s smile faded by degrees, the way she’d learned to let things fade instead of drop. “I see.”

“Not bad things,” the girl said quickly. “Just talking.”

“What sort of talking?”

But the girl had lost her nerve, or remembered an errand. She was already off down the path, bare feet slapping the packed dirt, one hand still raised in a small backward wave.

Goci shut the door and leaned against it, the basket cool and heavy in her arms. She had been in Dandani only a few hours, yet people were already discussing her before she’d unpacked a comb.

She set the basket on the table and went back to the window. The lanterns were flickering in a wind she couldn’t feel indoors, and the chimney smoke leaned west. She looked toward the mountains, black now against a darker sky, and something up there was looking back. It wasn’t the town this time, or its neighbors with their gossip and baskets. It was older than that, and patient, and very far off, and yet no farther than the other side of a door she hadn’t known was in the room.

She couldn’t say what it meant. It was nothing, she told herself. A long road and an empty stomach could make a woman imagine anything in a strange house at dusk.

*  *  *

She ate bread from her satchel, soft cheese and three plums, standing at the window because she couldn’t yet make herself sit. By the time she finished, dark had settled over Dandani and the town had gone still. She thought of the man at the fountain, his “They stay,” the girl’s basket, and the warmth in the doorframe. Small oddities, she told herself. Nothing more.

She slept with the window open. She couldn’t have said why, when every habit she had should have made her bar it.

*  *  *

The next morning she walked the streets before the crowds, learning where things were. She told herself it wasn’t because she planned to stay, only that knowing a place made leaving easier, and leaving was what she always did. She found the bakery first, warm air rolling out of the open door thick with rising dough. Then the well, its stones worn pale by generations of hands and rope. Then a narrow lane climbed toward the palace. There she stopped.

The palace stood on a ridge of pale stone and caught the early light better than the rest of the town managed. Its towers were not tall and could not have rivaled the fortress-cities she had seen farther north, in the old life she didn’t let herself think about before noon, but they had been built to be looked at. Gold banners stirred in the morning wind, each bearing the King’s crest, a sun wrapped in a serpent’s coil. Goci had known powerful men, more than her share, and had learned at some cost that power depended less on what a man could actually do than on how completely everyone around him had agreed to believe it.

A young guard had noticed her looking. He held his spear a little too upright, as boys do with things they’ve only just been trusted with. “You shouldn’t stand there.”

“It’s a public road,” she said, mild as milk.

He looked embarrassed, and some of the stiffness went out of his shoulders. “I didn’t mean… it’s only that people don’t usually stop here to look.”

“I know.” She walked on without looking back, his eyes on her back longer than duty required.

At the edge of the palace district stood a row of old houses belonging to families who had served the crown for generations, small brass plaques beside each door, polished to a shine that spoke of pride rather than upkeep. Goci read them as she passed, names and dates and titles, a whole compressed history of loyalty and inheritance. She wondered how many people in Dandani spent their whole lives trying to belong to a history someone else had already written, generation folding into generation like pages in a book none of them had been asked to help author. She didn’t want that. She never had, even before she’d had reason to fear it. She wanted the opposite: a life no one remembered, a name that left no plaque, nothing for anyone to polish after she was gone.

At the market, Lily, the girl from the fruit basket, found her again. She appeared at Goci’s elbow the way children do when they’ve been watching for you. “You came back.”

“I live here. For now.” The words came more easily than they had the day before, as though saying them twice had begun to make them slightly true. Goci smiled despite herself. “You listen too much, I think.”

“My mother says listening is how you learn what people are hiding.” Lily said it proudly, as if it were a skill she’d worked at rather than nosiness with a motherly excuse.

“Your mother sounds dangerous.”

“She is.” Lily beamed, delighted, and fell into step beside her as if the matter were settled. They walked between the stalls, past baskets of new greens and a man selling clay whistles shaped like birds.

“Do you know everyone in Dandani?” Goci asked, half to make conversation and half, she admitted, because she wanted to know.

“Almost. My mother says in a town this small everybody’s business belongs to everybody else, whether they like it or not.”

“Then tell me something no one else knows.” She said it lightly, testing the shape of an ordinary conversation, one she’d almost forgotten how to have.

Lily chewed her lip and considered it with great seriousness. “Mr. Tovan keeps sweets under his counter. The good ones. Not the ones he sells.”

Goci laughed, a real laugh that surprised her. “That’s your great secret?”

“No.” Lily dropped her voice and looked left and right with theatrical caution. “That’s his secret. My secret is I know where he keeps them.”

Goci shook her head, smiling. The tightness in her shoulders eased. Then Lily went still, her small body drawing in like a bird that has felt a hawk’s shadow.

“What is it?”

Lily pointed to the tree line at the edge of the square, where the market gave way to forest. “There.”

A figure stood among the trees, too far off and too deep in shadow to make out. It was tall and motionless, facing the market. Facing her, and the certainty of it dropped through Goci’s stomach. Her smile was gone as though it had never been. She took one step forward, on instinct, and the figure wasn’t there. It hadn’t walked away or stepped back into deeper shadow. It was simply gone, like a reflection when you turn from the water.

“Did you see that?”

Lily nodded, wide-eyed, all her showman’s bravado gone.

“Who was it?”

“I don’t know.” The girl’s voice had gone very small.

Goci stared at the ordinary green shadow where the figure had stood. The certainty came before any explanation: she had seen it before, though not in Dandani and not in this life. In a dream, perhaps, or some half-asleep hour she had never examined closely. She put the thought away with the others, in the locked room where she kept the truth about the silver-threaded shawl and why she had left her last home in the middle of the night with nothing but a satchel.

That night she laid the shawl beside her bed instead of folding it into the wardrobe as she had every night before. She didn’t know why. Some old, wordless instinct said she might need it close, and soon.

She woke to children laughing beyond the cottage. At first she thought it was a dream, but the voices carried clearly: children allowed to stay up past the hour when sensible adults would have sent them to bed. She went to the window. Across the square, an orange glow moved between the trees. She couldn’t see who sat around it, only firelight and shadows passing through it. Then an old voice carried across the night. Someone was telling a story. She couldn’t make out the words, but the listeners knew it already and still expected to be surprised. She stayed at the window longer than she meant to. This was nothing like the anonymous town she’d imagined. Yet the sound bothered her less than it should have. It reminded her of something she couldn’t remember.

*  *  *

The next afternoon she found the library. It was near the southern square, two rooms above an old tailor’s shop, reached by a narrow outside stair that creaked under her weight. Through the low door came the hush of a place built for reading, and the smell of dust, cedar and old paper that she had loved, unreasonably, all her life. A woman named Sera sat behind the desk mending the spine of a slim volume with a needle so fine it was nearly invisible in her fingers.

The sign over the door had once been painted blue, though most of the letters had faded: THE HOUSE OF REMEMBERING. Below it, nearly lost to age, someone had carved the same circle-and-line symbol Goci had seen beside her cottage door.

She stopped on the stair. A man came down past her with a bundle of reeds, glanced up at the sign, and smiled. “You found it.”

“I was looking for the library.”

He shrugged. “People who come here looking for books usually find the House instead.” He went off into the market, and Goci looked at the sign a while longer before she climbed the rest of the steps.

“Looking for something?” Sera asked without looking up, in the tone of someone who had asked the question so often it had worn smooth.

“History.”

Sera studied her briefly. “Then you’ve come to the wrong shelf.”

Goci glanced at the rows of books. “I thought this was a library.”

“It is.”

“That sounds contradictory.”

“Only if you think history is what people write down.” Sera put aside the book she was mending. “In Dandani we keep three kinds of memory. What was written. What was made. What was told.”

“Which one is true?”

Sera smiled. “That’s why we keep all three.”

The answer was so simple she could have laughed. She looked around the room again and the room rearranged itself. Books, yes, but also a narrow shelf of clay tablets marked with patterns instead of words, and strips of faded woven cloth hanging from wooden pegs, each carrying small repeated symbols. A rolled map lay in a glass case. Along one wall stood carved pieces of wood that looked, at first glance, like ordinary household things.

“They don’t look like books,” Goci said.

“Most memories don’t.” Sera got up and went toward a narrow doorway at the back. “Come.”

Goci hesitated. “You said history.”

“I did. Now I’ll show you why Dandani has lasted as long as it has.”

The doorway opened onto a smaller room, colder than the first and windowless. Shelves climbed the walls from floor to ceiling, holding bundles of cloth, wooden tablets, sealed jars, carved stones, folded maps, and objects Goci couldn’t guess the use of. In the middle stood a long table, its surface covered in hundreds of small marks. The marks weren’t writing. They were names. Some were sharp and clear; others had been rubbed nearly smooth.

“Whose names are these?”

Sera didn’t answer at once. “People who came here.”

“And?”

“Some stayed.”

Goci looked again. “And some didn’t?”

Sera’s face changed. “Everyone leaves Dandani eventually.” The words were ordinary. The way she said them was not.

Goci stepped closer. Near the edge of the table one name had been scratched through so hard that the wood beneath it had split. “Why keep—”

“Because being forgotten isn’t the same as being gone.” Sera laid her hand on the table’s edge. “It’s the oldest promise in this house: that someone remembers.”

The words went through Goci, and she didn’t know why. For the first time, the House of Remembering felt less like a library than a place where the town kept its absences.

Back in the main room she browsed slowly, trailing a finger along cracked spines. Most of the books were about Limodi State, its kings and wars and harvests and treaties, the long dull record of a kingdom mostly at peace with itself, which she supposed was a blessing few kingdoms got. Then, tucked sideways behind a row of taller volumes as though someone had wanted it kept and hidden at once, she found a thin book with no title on its spine. She drew it out. Inside were drawings: circles within circles, lines crossing at angles that made her eyes want to slide off them, the same restless symbols carved into window frames all over town. Her pulse quickened, a small cold thrill she didn’t look at closely.

“Where did you get this?” She kept her voice as idle as she could manage.

Sera glanced over. “That old thing? Donated years ago, before my time. I’ve never had the heart to throw it out, though half the town thinks I should.”

“Who donated it?”

“I don’t remember. Nobody does, I think, or if they do they’ve never told me.”

Goci turned past the diagrams to a page showing two figures standing on opposite sides of a thin silver line. It had been drawn with more care than the rest, as if this one page had mattered more to whoever made it. Beneath it, in an old-fashioned hand, were three words: The veil remembers.

She shut the book, maybe too fast. “Can I borrow it?”

Sera shook her head, not unkindly, but with a firmness that surprised Goci. “No.”

“Why not?”

“Nobody borrows that book. It’s a rule of the place, older than me. I only keep it.”

“That’s a strange rule for a library.”

Sera’s face, light and easy until now, turned thoughtful, and something serious moved behind her eyes. “Because it always comes back,” she said. “Whether the person meant it to or not.”

Goci stared, waiting for the joke to land, for Sera to grin and make the whole exchange ordinary again. Sera did laugh, a moment too late. “I’m joking, of course.” Neither of them smiled. The joke, if it had been one, sat between them in the room.

Sera watched her. “You’ve seen that symbol before.”

It wasn’t a question. Goci’s fingers tightened on the book. “No.”

“Then perhaps you’ve seen what it remembers.”

Goci looked at her sharply. Sera’s expression softened. “I didn’t say it remembered you.”

“Could it?”

“I didn’t say that either.” She took the book gently out of Goci’s hands. “Some questions turn dangerous when they’re asked before a person is ready for the answer.”

“I didn’t come here for dangerous questions.”

“No,” Sera said. “You came here to disappear.”

Goci went very still. Sera slid the book back into its place. “Dandani has a strange habit. It rarely gives people what they come looking for.”

The old pressure stirred under Goci’s ribs. “Then what does it give them?”

Sera met her eyes. “That depends on what they’ve been running from.”

Goci turned to go, and glanced back, and for an instant so brief she could never afterward be sure of it, the spine of the little book shimmered, like air above a fire on a hot day. She went out into the ordinary brightness of the afternoon. The sun was low and Dandani looked peaceful again, gilded and unremarkable, a town that appeared on no maps that mattered and troubled the sleep of no king. But a new certainty had lodged beneath her ribs, alongside the older weight she carried: the town had secrets, and she had arrived with one of her own.

*  *  *

That evening she sat at her window with tea going lukewarm in her hands and learned Dandani’s sounds: the baker’s shutters clapping twice, the fountain that was dry by day and perhaps not silent at night, palace bells marking hours she hadn’t learned to count, children called home one by one. The streets emptied into the same stillness she’d felt on her first night.

Later, unable to sleep, she followed the voices she’d heard the night before.

The fire was closer this time. It burned in a clearing beyond the southern houses, ringed by children cross-legged on woven mats, with older people behind them on low stools or straight on the ground. Nobody was in a hurry. Nobody had a book. At the center sat a woman with white hair braided down her back, telling a story about a woman who had walked into Dandani with nothing but a silver-threaded shawl and a name she no longer trusted.

Goci stopped behind a tree.

The children leaned in. “And what happened to her?” one of them asked.

The old woman smiled. “That depends which version you hear.”

A boy frowned. “There are different versions?”

“There are always different versions.”

“Then which one’s true?”

The woman looked into the fire. “The one that teaches you something you didn’t know you needed to learn.” Several of the children groaned, and Goci nearly smiled.

The old woman went on. “Long ago, before the palace stood above the town, before the road was a road, before the river had the name you know today, there was a boundary here.”

The laughter stopped. “A boundary between what?” a child asked, low.

The woman glanced toward the mountains. “Between what could be seen and what couldn’t. The people of Dandani were told never to cross it. So naturally, someone did.”

A ripple of laughter ran through the children. “What happened?”

“The world changed.”

“How?”

She leaned closer to the fire. “That’s the part the elders disagree about.”

Goci listened, but soon she couldn’t hear the next words. The pressure had come back under her ribs, and this time it wasn’t warm. It was recognition. She stepped backward, and a twig cracked under her heel.

Every child turned. The old woman looked straight at her. No one spoke, and then the storyteller smiled. “Ah,” she said softly. “There you are.”

Goci couldn’t tell whether the words were meant for her, but the old woman plainly knew. And the fire, impossibly, burned a little brighter.

Back at the cottage she sat awake in the dark and took stock of how little of her plan had survived three days. She had meant to stay outside all this, a stone dropped in water that left no ripple. But Lily had given her fruit before she’d unpacked, and told her secrets a child ought to save for an older friend. Mara had handed over a key and a warning about bells she still didn’t understand. The baker, unasked, had already learned how she took her tea and made a small private ceremony of getting it right. It should have frightened her. It was exactly the kind of entanglement she had spent years avoiding, the soft threads by which a place gets its hooks into you before you notice you’ve stopped planning to leave. Instead it felt strangely comforting, and she didn’t entirely trust that.

She had spent years believing belonging cost you something: a name, a history, a version of yourself you couldn’t take back once it was known. Being known without being owned. She turned the idea over and didn’t believe it. She watched the last lanterns burn low along the empty street, and then she shut the window against the night.
